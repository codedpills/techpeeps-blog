# A Good Software Developer Is Only as Good if They Can Sell Their Product

There is a line Isaac dropped mid-conversation that stuck with me long after we stopped recording. Talking about the strange, in-between nature of his work, he put it plainly: "a good software developer is only as good if they can sell their product," and, he added, "a good salesperson is only as good if their products are good." That is his whole job in a sentence. It also happens to be the reason his profile is a little different from most of the software engineers who pass through this show.

Isaac is a customer engineer at Google Cloud, based in Atlanta, Georgia, where he told me the winter had finally warmed up after a rough couple of weeks. Originally from Accra, Ghana, he sits in that fascinating space where technical depth meets the softer art of helping someone decide to spend real money. If you have ever wondered what a "customer success engineer" actually does all day, this one is for you.

## So what is a customer engineer, really?

I asked the obvious question first, mostly on behalf of anyone else who has seen the title floating around LinkedIn and never quite decoded it. Isaac reached for an analogy he clearly uses a lot: a customer engineer is "a technical liaison within a given engagement," and those engagements are usually sales engagements.

The picture he painted goes like this. Companies, startups, and individuals want to buy services on the cloud. Most people, he pointed out, "usually think about storage and storing devices," but the cloud is much wider than that: data analytics, compute, a whole spread of services. Sellers do the selling, but somewhere in the middle you need someone who can translate the jargon between the buyer and the seller. That someone is the customer engineer.

He walked me through a concrete example. If a startup wants to build something for data analytics, the customer engineer runs "that initial discovery call to see what does this customer want to build," then helps map where they see themselves in two months, six months, a year, and capacity plans for it. His summary of the role: "you are like a technical guide, a guardian, a consultant, but you don't necessarily implement everything."

## Talking to a CEO on one call, a data scientist on the next

What makes it tricky, he explained, is the range of people on the other end of the line. Most are technical, but not all. "You could be talking on one call to a CEO and the next call is a data scientist," he said, and the way you frame a technology has to shift accordingly. Because at the end of the day, he reminded me, "you are influencing a purchasing decision," and "technical jargon can only go so far."

I wanted to know how this differs from roles like developer advocate or developer relations, which spin up under a dozen names. Isaac's distinction was clean. Customer engineering is "mainly focused on one-to-one customer engagements," things like thirty-minute sales calls and follow-up emails. A developer advocate, by contrast, is "more community focused," evangelizing a particular tool through videos and events. He knew this from experience: at Twilio, where he interned, "there was always like a huge emphasis on education and educating developers on a particular API."

## The balance: how technical do you actually need to be?

This was the question I most wanted answered. Where does the technical end and the sales begin? Isaac called it "a really good question" and did not pretend there was a tidy formula.

You do need to be genuinely technical, he was firm on that: "you can only speak to something you've tried or know how it works." But he has met customer engineers who lean far more sales-centric. The thing that actually carries you, in his telling, is neither skill in isolation. It is the discovery work: "What does this customer want? Why are they here? Where do they want to go?" He described it as "a weird combination of customer empathy" fused with the technical knowledge you have accumulated.

Then came that line again, the one that anchors the whole role. The good developer needs to be able to sell; the good salesperson needs a good product. "So it's kind of like the best of both worlds in my role," he said. Coming from someone who spends his days in that overlap, it did not sound like a cliché.

## A day in the life

Readers who like knowing what the calendar actually looks like will appreciate Isaac's breakdown. Much of his day is spent managing a "book of business" alongside sellers, analyzing quarterly metrics to see how accounts are doing on spend. Then come the customer calls, "usually like 30-minute calls spread across my day," ranging from intro conversations to deep technical discovery. Sometimes a customer wants an architecture review of a database or AI system, and he explains which Google Cloud products fit.

His rough split: about 60% customer engagement (emails, video calls, the occasional phone call), around 20% preparing for larger-scale events his company runs monthly or quarterly, and the last 20% on his own enablement. That last slice matters more than it sounds, especially now. "In the realm of AI, things are changing every week or every day," he said, "so I have to make sure I'm educated and I can apply this to my engagement."

## Getting in the door

For anyone eyeing a role like this, Isaac was generous with the mechanics. His own path started with a LinkedIn message from a recruiter at the tail end of his Twilio internship, for a Google program called the Cloud Technical Residency. He described it as a role "designed to train people to become technical sellers," built for graduates who have the technical knowledge "but they don't have a lot of business savvy, sales rapport." The interview was four rounds spanning the spectrum: "how does the internet work," protocols, architecture, sales-specific questions, and coding too.

His resume advice circled back to impact. Frame your experiences "around how you've enabled customers and what impact you've had," and reach for metrics: if you led a team, is there a percentage that shows you "moved something forward"? He flagged his tutoring and community-building work as things worth highlighting, because the technology stack is good, but showing how you influence a community is what differentiates you. And he was honest about projects: coding projects, yes, but also evidence you have built solutions "that have affected communities."

On compensation, he stayed careful and precise. It varies by company, partly because "sales teams have to change and adapt to different economic climates" in a way software methodologies like Agile do not. Some customer engineers sit on a sales plan with a base salary plus deal-based compensation. Isaac, for the record, has been on a company plan: "I wasn't compensated for closing deals." He declined to speak to the sales-plan structure directly, since it is a position he has "never been in." I appreciated the restraint. It is rare and refreshing when someone only claims what they actually know.

## Rewinding: one year at KNUST, then Miami

I wanted to understand how he got here, so we rewound. Isaac did his first year of university at KNUST in Ghana before transferring to the University of Miami in 2018. He remembered KNUST fondly, especially the communities "focused around enabling students to learn about technology," and clubs like Hack Lab. He was candid too about the challenges of scale, sitting in classes of "like 250 students."

Leaving was always part of the plan. He wanted the exposure, the pipeline, and proximity to "a lot of the companies I learned about growing up." He had even taken the SATs while at KNUST, keeping the option warm. "If an opportunity opened up, I will take it," he said. And when it did, he took it.

## Scaling a coding course from 20 students to 1,000

One thread I loved was the story of a STEM bootcamp he joined in 2017, Project IceWest, run at the University of Ghana through the Nseta Foundation. They taught programming with a language called Processing, adapted so students could design games on their phones, not just laptops.

The interesting part was the scaling. From 2018 onward, Isaac helped take the course online, and by 2020 they ran a cohort of about 1,000 students across Africa. At that scale, he told me, "we had to literally design scripts to grade students' assignments because the few of us couldn't grade all of those things." About 700 students finished, which, as he noted, "for an online course, that was really impressive." Watching it grow "from having 20 students in like 2017" to a thousand three years later taught him "how products grow with scale."

That resonated with me personally. I had my own in-person bootcamp experience when COVID hit, and the scramble to move everything online, keep engagement alive, and figure out grading was its own kind of trial by fire. Different approaches, same fire.

## The challenges nobody puts in the highlight reel

It sounds rosy when you lay it out as a timeline. Isaac was clear that it was not. The first shift was intellectual. Ghanaian schooling, he reflected, is very "Q and A," heavy on memorizing enough "to be able to do on the exams." That framework has its place, but it left him under-equipped for environments built around implementing and collaborating. People skills, he felt, "are not as emphasized" until he arrived in the US and had to "talk about yourself and how that relates to the work you're doing."

Then there was culture shock, which he named as "definitely the height of everything." Miami was the first place he had been where most people "weren't like Ghanaian," surrounded by different worldviews and ways of interacting. What carried him through was "being around community who is supportive of you," friends, teachers, professors, advisors.

And there was the hard, structural reality of being an immigrant. "You are competing against a lot of people who are looking for jobs," he said, and you need employers who will sponsor you. Getting sponsorship was "one of the biggest reasons why it took me a long time to even get an internship." It worked out. But it was not smooth, and he did not pretend otherwise.

## His advice: skills, preparation, and community

When I asked what he would tell people wanting to break into a role like his, Isaac's answer had three clear pillars, delivered against the backdrop of what he called "hysteria around like AI replacing jobs."

First, breadth. Job markets hire for specific skills, and those targets shift with the economy, so aim for "a broad range of skills," from talking to people to explaining technical concepts to understanding business problems. He offered a practical method: work in "focus bursts," dedicating two or three weeks to one thing rather than doing everything at once. And he pointed to his own life as proof it compounds unexpectedly: "I didn't know that being a tutor would help me so much in customer empathy."

Second, preparation. He admitted he "lost a lot of opportunities" by entering them "in a lackluster manner." His fix was disciplined and unglamorous: updating LinkedIn with classes and projects long before it earned "a lot of the clicks and the hype," building the muscle of "personal branding and personal building."

Third, community and putting yourself out there. "A lot of people shoot themselves in the foot by not actually applying for opportunities," he said, but he was equally firm that "you also can't do it alone." Especially, he added, "when you have to be in a field or a career where you might be one of the only people or the only minority involved."

## Where to find him

Isaac is generous with his time and clearly thinking about how to "do more to enable more people who are interested in fields like that." The best place to reach him is LinkedIn, where he posts about Google Cloud initiatives and events "both abroad and back home." If you have questions, that is his door.

What stays with me from this conversation is how much of Isaac's success was built in the overlap: between technical and sales, between Accra and Atlanta, between memorizing and building, between doing it alone and leaning on community. He lives in the in-between and has made it a career. The world of technical selling is a little clearer, and a little warmer, for having someone like him explaining it.
